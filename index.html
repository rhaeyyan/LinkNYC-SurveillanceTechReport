The Big Apple's
Big Brother Problem.

LinkNYC was sold to New York City as a digital equity programme — free gigabit Wi-Fi for the boroughs that needed it most. What it built instead was the largest warrantless surveillance network in the five boroughs, concentrated in the neighbourhoods least positioned to push back.

The Background

They replaced the payphones. Then they started watching.

Starting in 2015, New York City began replacing its remaining payphones with tall steel kiosks — 9.5-foot columns with large touchscreens, USB charging ports, and the promise of free gigabit Wi-Fi. The pitch was straightforward: close the digital divide. Give residents who couldn't afford home broadband a connection to the internet. The program was called LinkNYC, and by the time the first wave of deployments was complete it had become the largest public Wi-Fi network in the United States.

The company operating it is CityBridge LLC — not a city agency, but a private consortium assembled specifically to win the franchise contract. Its members are Intersection, the advertising and data company that manages the kiosks' commercial inventory; Qualcomm, which built the hardware; and Boldyn Networks, which runs the infrastructure. Three corporations, one franchise agreement, and the personal data of millions of New Yorkers moving through their systems every day — passively, continuously, and without any of them being asked.

Under the POST Act, the NYPD is legally required to publish detailed impact and use policies for every surveillance tool it operates: who can access the data, under what conditions, retained for how long. CityBridge is not the NYPD. None of that applies. The kiosks run under a franchise agreement negotiated in 2014, before most of their surveillance capabilities were apparent to anyone not building them. No mandatory disclosures. No public audit cycle. No enforcement mechanism. The city built a surveillance network operating entirely outside the accountability framework it wrote for itself. That isn't a loophole someone missed. That's the contract.

The Finding

The NYPD needs a court order to do what LinkNYC does by default.

The NYPD publishes an official Impact & Use Policy for "WiFi Geolocation Tracking Devices." It requires probable cause and a court order. LinkNYC — the largest Wi-Fi geolocation network in the city — requires neither. Same underlying capability. A completely different set of rules. Or rather, no rules at all.

The Regulatory Gap

The POST Act requires the NYPD to disclose the surveillance tools it owns. CityBridge isn't the NYPD — it's a private consortium — which means the 2,255+ kiosks logging device identifiers across the five boroughs sit entirely outside that disclosure framework. Yet the footage and metadata they collect flow voluntarily into the Domain Awareness System — a network of 80,000+ cameras, five million license plate scans per day, drone feeds, and 911 call records, all fused by machine learning into “entity reports” on every New Yorker: appearance, social affiliations, 311 call history, sealed arrest records, DNA. Accessible from any officer’s smartphone. From there, as documented, to federal agencies that answer to no one in this city. The city gets the data without the accountability. That's not an oversight. It's the architecture.

The Machine

You don't have to connect. Walking past is enough.

LinkNYC replaced New York's 13,000 obsolete payphones starting in 2015, promising free gigabit Wi-Fi, charging ports, and domestic calls. The pitch was digital equity. The funding mechanism was something else entirely.

The programme is built and operated at no direct cost to taxpayers — because it runs on advertising. Intersection, the ad arm of the CityBridge consortium, pitches brands a "context-aware platform" for reaching consumers as they move through physical space. Their own marketing materials promise to identify niches like "18-to-34-year-old high-income college graduates" based on behavioural data harvested from the surrounding environment. To deliver that promise, every kiosk has to function as a sensor hub first and a public utility second.

Each structure carries dual HD cameras, Wi-Fi access points, Bluetooth beacons, and environmental sensors monitoring noise, temperature, light, and air quality. The cameras retain footage for seven days. The beacons continuously scan for nearby devices, logging the unique MAC address your phone broadcasts whenever Wi-Fi is enabled — regardless of whether you've ever touched the kiosk or even noticed it standing there. The 32-foot Link5G poles that began appearing in 2022 extend that reach further, adding 5G antennae and greater sensor capacity. At some point, it stops being street furniture and starts being something else entirely.

01 / The Original Sin

Manhattan first. Outer boroughs last.

The Link 1.0 rollout concentrated kiosks in high-income Manhattan commercial corridors — not because that's where the digital divide was widest, but because that's where Intersection's advertisers wanted their inventory. Equity was the stated purpose. Ad revenue was the actual logic.

Less than 10% of the original LinkNYC footprint went to the Bronx — the borough with the highest rates of digital disconnection and poverty in the city. Spatial researchers concluded that the programme "completely failed its target population." That's a measured way of saying the people most in need of public Wi-Fi were last in line for it.

02 / The Pivot

Near-bankruptcy triggered the outer-borough mandate.

By 2019, CityBridge owed the city nearly $70 million in arrears. Rather than rethinking the franchise, the city renegotiated it — restructuring the debt in exchange for a new generation of hardware: the towering 32-foot Link5G poles. An equity mandate came with them. 90% of all new Link5G units must be built in the outer boroughs or above 96th Street in Manhattan.

739 specific kiosk locations were mandated across 13 community districts selected for low median income, severe broadband gaps, and absence of existing LinkNYC infrastructure. The surveillance follows the need.

The Confession

The city finally looked. Here's what it found.

At some point, the city had to look. In 2021, the Office of Technology and Innovation commissioned KPMG to audit CityBridge's compliance with its own privacy obligations. The resulting report was uploaded to a government page in January 2023 — heavily redacted, no press release, no link from the LinkNYC portal. The NYCLU found it six months later.

PF-01 — Critical Finding

For years, CityBridge stored your device's fingerprint in plaintext.

A MAC address is a unique, hardcoded identifier your phone broadcasts constantly — it's effectively a fingerprint for your device. CityBridge was contractually required to anonymize these before they were ever stored. They didn't. For over half a decade, every device that came within range of a kiosk was uniquely identified and logged, in direct violation of the franchise agreement and the company's own privacy policy. The fix — a one-way hash applied to stored addresses — wasn't deployed until December 2022. Years into the network's operation.

PF-02

The Wi-Fi sign-on page showed Intersection's privacy policy — the ad company's — instead of LinkNYC's. Users were legally misled about who governed their data.

PF-03

Welcome emails lacked the legally required unsubscribe link, trapping users in marketing communications in violation of anti-spam law.

PF-04

The Wi-Fi splash page failed to reference the correct Terms of Use and Privacy Policy — leaving users without clear notice of what they were accepting.

KPMG audit (OTI, redacted Jan 2023); CityBridge remediation letter to OTI, 12 Dec 2022; NYCLU testimony to NYC Council Tech Committee, 7 Jun 2023.

Who Pays

Privacy is a luxury. The poor pay with theirs.

Wealthier residents can largely ignore LinkNYC — they have private broadband, unlimited data plans, and no real reason to use a public kiosk. Low-income residents, undocumented immigrants, and unhoused New Yorkers who depend on public Wi-Fi as a digital lifeline don't have that option. For them, the network isn't a convenience; it's infrastructure they can't opt out of. And the price of access is their civil liberties.

Academic literature calls this digital redlining — a system where access is finally extended to underserved communities, but only bundled with invasive monitoring. The Link5G mandate in the 13 priority districts makes this explicit in the contract itself: the same neighbourhoods with the highest broadband deficit, the lowest incomes, and the longest history of aggressive policing are precisely the ones designated to receive the next wave of surveillance infrastructure. It isn't a coincidence. It's the deal.

That dependency is not accidental, and the city has proved it knows how to exploit it. In 2025, Mayor Adams secretly connected NYCHA security cameras to the Domain Awareness System using NYCHA’s own WiFi infrastructure — without informing residents, City Council, or public housing administrators. One campus was already live-streaming directly to NYPD smartphones. Plans existed to add nineteen more. NYPD officers had previously admitted watching tenants “for entertainment.” Free internet, delivered at last — and used to build a live surveillance feed into the homes of the city’s poorest residents.

S.T.O.P. documented what that exposure means in practice. ICE has accessed NYPD license plate reader data. Sealed records — records legally protected from disclosure — were shared with federal agents and used against a pro-Palestinian protester in deportation proceedings. Private contractors simultaneously advise city agencies and ICE's enforcement operations. The data doesn't need to travel far. The people who manage it already do. For undocumented New Yorkers who rely on LinkNYC as their primary internet connection, that is not an abstraction.

This isn't unique to New York. Researchers tracking the rollout of InLinkUK kiosks in London's Tower Hamlets documented the same pattern: smart kiosks deployed in marginalised neighbourhoods catalysed heavier policing operations and data-driven criminalisation. Different city. Different branding. The same architecture producing the same outcome — because the incentives that built it were the same to begin with.

What Can Be Done

There is a bill. It needs voices.

There is a bill. Two legislators have introduced the Digital Fairness Act in both chambers of the New York State Legislature, and it is in committee right now — which means it can still move this session. What it needs isn't money or political connections. It needs constituent contact. A message from a voter in a district is one of the few things that actually moves a committee vote.

●  Pending — In Committee
The Digital Fairness Act
Senate Bill S4276  ·  Assembly Bill A3308  ·  Introduced Feb 2025  ·  Committee on Internet & Technology

The bill requires any entity processing the personal data of 500+ New Yorkers to provide meaningful, affirmative notice before collection begins. It bans discriminatory targeted advertising and establishes accountability for automated decision systems in government and business contexts.

  • Closes the consent gap: opt-in required before passive MAC tracking — directly addressing KPMG finding PF-01
  • Covers the POST Act loophole: applies to private contractors like CityBridge, not just NYPD-owned tools
  • Algorithmic accountability: the same systems used to target ads can deny housing, credit, and employment
  • NYCLU has published a formal legislative memo in support
  • Legislative momentum: the NY Senate advanced a companion surveillance-pricing bill (S8623) in May 2026 — the same political current

Tell your rep to pass S4276 / A3308.
