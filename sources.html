Methodology & Citations

Sources & Bibliography

Every factual claim in “Surveilled by Default: How NYC's Free Wi-Fi Became a Surveillance Network” traces to a primary document, an independently reviewed audit, an academic paper, or on-the-record reporting. This page lists every source cited in the report, grouped by type, with a note on what each supports and a link back to where it appears. Government filings, litigation records, and audit PDFs were downloaded and read directly rather than taken from secondary summaries; where a claim depends on characterizing a source's argument rather than a fact it states outright, the note below says so.

§ 01

Legislation & Public Policy

6 sources
  1. Digital Fairness Act — Senate Bill S4276 Legislation
    New York State Senate · introduced Feb 2025 · in Committee on Internet & Technology · no Assembly companion yet

    The pending state bill this report calls on readers to support. Would require affirmative, opt-in consent before a business collects personal data from 500+ New York residents — closing the contractor loophole that exempts CityBridge from the POST Act. As of publication, no companion bill has been introduced in the Assembly; an earlier version of this report incorrectly cited a carried-over bill number, A3308, from the expired 2023-2024 session.

    Cited in: The Fix Act Now
  2. NYPD Impact & Use Policy — “WiFi Geolocation Tracking Devices” (POST Act) Government
    NYPD / NYC.gov · POST Act public disclosures

    Establishes the probable-cause-and-court-order standard the NYPD must meet before deploying a Wi-Fi geolocation device. This is the legal baseline LinkNYC's kiosks operate without, and the basis for the report's central comparison.

    Cited in: Background The Finding
  3. NYC Internet Master Plan Government
    NYC Office of the Chief Technology Officer · Jan 2020

    Source of the city's own “Internet Deserts” intervention-zone map, later shown to closely mirror the districts mandated for Link5G deployment.

    Cited in: Who Pays
  4. Link5G Design Proposal & Briefing Government
    NYC Public Design Commission / DoITT · Oct 2021

    The city's own presentation on Link5G tower design, including the technical rationale for the 32-foot height and the per-district equity-mandate kiosk counts.

    Cited in: The Machine Who Pays
  5. Big Apple Connect Government
    NYC Mayor's Office · program extension announced Sep 2025

    Documents a sensor-free municipal broadband alternative already serving roughly 330,000 NYCHA residents — evidence the city has a working model for public Wi-Fi that isn't bundled with ambient data collection.

    Cited in: Who Pays
  6. LinkNYC Kiosk Locations (dataset s4kf-3yrf) Data
    NYC Open Data · live feed

    Powers the report's live kiosk count and interactive map. Because this feed updates continuously, its figures will differ from the static counts cited in the surrounding prose.

    Cited in: The Map
§ 02

Franchise, Audits & Oversight

4 sources
  1. CityBridge / LinkNYC Privacy Policy Corporate · Legal
    CityBridge LLC · as published

    The operative legal document governing kiosk data collection, and the report's most-cited primary source — the basis for its central comparison to the NYPD's POST Act standard and for the finding that camera footage and passively collected data can be shared with law enforcement without a warrant requirement.

    Cited in: Background The Finding The Machine
  2. LinkNYC Technical Audit Report — Privacy Section Audit
    KPMG, commissioned by NYC Office of Technology & Innovation · fieldwork Jul–Dec 2021 · posted redacted Jan 2023

    Source of findings PF-01 through PF-04. Downloaded and read directly against this report's claims: PF-01 found MAC addresses were encrypted but not anonymized after a mid-2020/2021 vendor transition, not stored in plaintext as an earlier draft of this report incorrectly stated.

    Cited in: The Audit
  3. CityBridge Privacy Audit Remediation Response Corporate
    CityBridge LLC, letter to OTI · 12 Dec 2022

    CityBridge's formal response to the KPMG audit, asserting all four privacy findings had been remediated.

    Cited in: The Audit
  4. LinkNYC Program Revenues and Monitoring (Follow-Up) Audit
    Office of the New York State Comptroller · Sep 2023

    State-level financial and oversight audit of the LinkNYC program, cited for franchise-compliance and revenue context surrounding the 2019–2021 renegotiation.

    Cited in: The Map
§ 03

Civil-Liberties & Advocacy

10 sources
  1. Testimony Regarding Technology Oversight Hearing on LinkNYC Advocacy
    New York Civil Liberties Union · Nov 18, 2016

    The NYCLU's earliest formal objection to LinkNYC, filed the same year the network launched. Source for the finding that the original privacy policy permitted data retention “essentially indefinitely.”

    Cited in: The Finding
  2. Testimony Regarding the Oversight of LinkNYC Advocacy
    New York Civil Liberties Union (Daniel Schwarz) · Jun 7, 2023

    Presented the KPMG audit's findings at a City Council hearing, six months after OTI quietly posted the redacted report, and notes it took “many months and repeated outside pressure” before OTI released it at all. Not to be confused with the NYCLU's 2016 testimony above — the two share a near-identical URL.

    Cited in: The Audit
  3. Comments of the Surveillance Resistance Lab, NYC Council LinkNYC Hearing Advocacy
    Surveillance Resistance Lab · Jun 7, 2023

    Submitted at the same hearing as the NYCLU's 2023 testimony; provides a detailed policy history of LinkNYC and quotes the KPMG audit's PF-01 finding directly.

    Cited in: The Audit
  4. “LinkNYC is a Privacy Disaster. Here's Why.” Advocacy
    New York Civil Liberties Union · commentary

    Source of the “barely mitigated privacy disaster” characterization quoted in the report, and of the NYCLU's review of Intersection's advertiser-facing marketing materials describing audience segments by age, education, and income.

    Cited in: The Machine
  5. Legislative Memo — Digital Fairness Act Advocacy
    New York Civil Liberties Union

    The organization's formal memo of support for S4276, cited for the bill's committee status and the case for its passage.

    Cited in: The Fix
  6. “Dragnet City: NYPD's Omnipresent Domain Awareness System” Advocacy
    Surveillance Technology Oversight Project (S.T.O.P.) · Oct 2025

    Documents how data collected across the city's surveillance apparatus feeds the NYPD's Domain Awareness System and flows onward to federal agencies, including U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement.

    Cited in: The Finding Who Pays
  7. “Dear Mamdani: Dismantle NYC's Surveillance State” Advocacy
    Surveillance Technology Oversight Project (S.T.O.P.) · Feb 2026

    Source for the finding that NYCHA security cameras were connected to the Domain Awareness System without resident notification, and for documented ICE access to NYPD data involving sealed records.

    Cited in: Who Pays
  8. S.T.O.P. v. NYPD — Lawsuit Filing Legal
    Emery Celli Brinckerhoff Abady Ward & Maazel LLP, on behalf of S.T.O.P.

    Litigation challenging the legality of the Domain Awareness System, cited as background on the surveillance architecture that LinkNYC-derived data can ultimately feed into.

    Cited in: The Finding
  9. Black and Brown NYC Communities Disproportionately Exposed to Facial Recognition Surveillance Advocacy
    Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC)

    Independent research corroborating racial disparities in New York City's camera-based surveillance footprint, cited alongside Amnesty International's findings below.

    Cited in: Who Pays
  10. Decode Surveillance NYC: Facial Recognition Technology Reinforcing Racist Stop-and-Frisk Policing Advocacy
    Amnesty International · Feb 2022

    Crowdsourced mapping of more than 25,500 NYC CCTV cameras. Found that in the Bronx, Brooklyn, and Queens, the higher a neighborhood's proportion of non-white residents, the higher its concentration of facial-recognition-compatible cameras — the source for this report's claim that the Link5G mandate districts also carry histories of intensive policing.

    Cited in: Who Pays
§ 04

Academic Research

5 sources
  1. de Montjoye, Y.-A., et al. — “Unique in the Crowd: The Privacy Bounds of Human Mobility” Academic
    Scientific Reports (2013)

    Source of the statistic that four spatio-temporal points are sufficient to uniquely re-identify 95% of individuals in a mobility dataset — used throughout the report to explain the re-identification risk posed by ambient location data.

    Cited in: Hero The Machine
  2. “From Phone Booths to Wi-Fi Kiosks: The Spatial Inequality of Public Connectivity in New York City” Academic
    Technological Forecasting and Social Change (2025)

    Spatial-statistics analysis of the LinkNYC rollout, concluding that kiosk placement operated independently of broadband need and did not measurably reduce digital inequality.

    Cited in: The Map
  3. “Radio Frequency Fingerprint Identification for IoT: A Survey” Academic
    Security and Safety (2024)

    Documents that MAC-address randomization is an incomplete defense against tracking, because a device's radio hardware emits a distinguishable physical signature that persists across software-level identifier changes.

    Cited in: The Machine
  4. MobRFFI — “Non-cooperative Device Re-identification for Mobility Intelligence” Academic
    arXiv preprint (2025)

    Applies radio-frequency fingerprinting specifically to non-cooperative re-identification for mobility-intelligence applications — the category LinkNYC's advertising platform falls into.

    Cited in: The Machine
  5. “Digital Policing of Homeless and Other Marginalised Groups in Smart Cities” Academic
    Policing and Society (2025)

    Comparative case study tracking InLinkUK smart kiosks in London's Tower Hamlets, cited to show the correlation between kiosk deployment in lower-income neighborhoods and intensified data-driven policing is not unique to New York.

    Cited in: Who Pays
§ 05

Journalism

2 sources
  1. “Big Tech Pays to Supersize LinkNYC” Journalism
    THE CITY · Apr 2022

    Reporting on the 2021 franchise renegotiation and the shift to a mixed revenue model that leases pole space to cellular carriers as 5G antenna platforms.

    Cited in: The Map
  2. “NYC's Massive Link5G Towers Aren't Actually Providing 5G (Yet)” Journalism
    Gothamist · Jul 2024

    Found that only two of roughly 200 installed Link5G towers had carrier equipment fitted at the time of reporting, while the towers' sensor and Wi-Fi functions remained fully active throughout.

    Cited in: The Map
§ 06

Industry & Corporate

3 sources

Sources produced or commissioned by CityBridge or its consortium members. Used to represent the network's own claims about itself, not as independent verification.

  1. LinkNYC 2025 Impact Report — 10-Year Anniversary Corporate
    CityBridge / PR Newswire · 2025

    CityBridge's own usage figures, including the roughly 473,000 free phone calls facilitated monthly — cited to illustrate the population's structural reliance on the network for essential services like the EBT helpline.

    Cited in: Who Pays
  2. New Yorkers & Public Wi-Fi Survey Corporate
    HarrisX, commissioned by LinkNYC · May 2025

    Source of the 77%-support statistic for Link5G, cited alongside the survey's own disclosure that support strengthens only after respondents are read a description of the poles' benefits.

    Cited in: Who Pays
  3. Foundry Entity Resolution Corporate
    Palantir Technologies · product page

    Example of a commercial data-integration service capable of linking pseudonymous identifiers across datasets back to individuals — cited to explain why hashing a device identifier does not by itself guarantee anonymity.

    Cited in: The Audit
